# Research Workflow

## 1. Frame the question

Confirm or infer:

- exact claim or decision question;
- time horizon and information cutoff;
- relevant geography, market, population, or security;
- required depth;
- what outcome the research should enable.

Ask a question only when the missing information cannot be reasonably inferred. Otherwise proceed and state the assumption.

## 2. Decompose the problem

Use a claim tree rather than a topic list. A useful default is:

```text
What happened?
What is genuinely new?
Is it true?
What caused it?
How large is the effect?
Who captures it?
What evidence challenges it?
What remains unknown?
What decision follows?
```

For public-equity research, also test whether the apparent security implication is already expected.

## 3. Gather evidence

Start with the sources nearest the underlying fact. Use secondary or expert work to discover claims, interpret technical detail, or identify counterevidence, then trace load-bearing facts to originals where possible.

Search deliberately for:

- direct evidence;
- independent confirmation;
- contradictory evidence;
- historical or peer benchmarks;
- denominator and base-rate information;
- the strongest competing explanation.

## 4. Cross-check

For each important claim:

- identify the original source;
- assign an independence group;
- reproduce material calculations;
- check units, dates, denominators, definitions, and revisions;
- distinguish observed facts from company or expert interpretation;
- state whether evidence supports causality, correlation, or plausibility only.

## 5. Synthesize

A strong brief normally includes:

- executive verdict;
- corrected framing;
- findings by load-bearing claim;
- confidence and source limitations;
- counterfactual or competing explanation;
- implications for the decision;
- unresolved questions;
- next evidence and stopping decision.

Do not list every source equally. Emphasize the sources that change the conclusion.
